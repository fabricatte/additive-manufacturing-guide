Rapid prototyping has fundamentally changed how products move from concept to production. What once required weeks of tooling and thousands of dollars in setup costs now takes hours with a [3D printer](https://fabricatte.com). Among the dozens of additive manufacturing technologies available, **FDM (Fused Deposition Modeling)** and **SLA (Stereolithography)** remain the two most widely used for prototyping — and choosing between them can make or break your development timeline.

This guide breaks down when to use each technology, how they compare on the metrics that actually matter, and how to build a prototyping workflow that gets products to market faster.

## What Is Rapid Prototyping?

Rapid prototyping is the process of quickly fabricating physical models directly from CAD data to validate design concepts, test form and fit, evaluate ergonomics, and iterate before committing to production tooling. Unlike traditional prototyping methods — CNC machining, silicone molding, or handmade mockups — [3D printing](https://fabricatte.com) enables same-day turnaround with minimal setup.

The two dominant desktop and professional prototyping technologies each serve different purposes:

- **FDM** builds parts by extruding thermoplastic filament layer by layer
- **SLA** uses a UV laser to cure liquid photopolymer resin with exceptional precision

Neither is universally "better." The right choice depends entirely on what you're prototyping and why.

## FDM for Rapid Prototyping: The Workhorse

FDM is the most widely adopted 3D printing technology in the world, and for good reason. It's affordable, reliable, and works with engineering-grade thermoplastics that closely match production materials.

### When FDM Excels

- **Functional prototypes** — Testing mechanical properties, snap-fits, load-bearing structures, and moving assemblies
- **Large parts** — Build volumes up to 300×300×400mm on prosumer machines, even larger on industrial platforms
- **Material matching** — Prototype in the same material you'll use for production (ABS, PETG, nylon, polycarbonate, TPU)
- **Quick concept models** — Draft-quality prints in under an hour for basic geometry validation
- **Cost-sensitive projects** — Material costs of $20–50/kg make iteration cheap

### FDM Strengths for Prototyping

| Metric | FDM Performance |
|--------|----------------|
| Material cost | $20–50/kg |
| Typical layer height | 0.1–0.3mm |
| Build speed | 50–150mm/s |
| Material variety | 50+ engineering thermoplastics |
| Post-processing | Minimal (support removal, optional sanding) |

### FDM Limitations to Know

- Visible layer lines on surfaces (0.1mm layers are noticeable up close)
- Anisotropic strength — parts are weaker along the Z-axis
- Support removal can leave marks on complex geometries
- Fine details below 0.8mm become unreliable

For [functional prototyping and production parts](https://fabricatte.com), FDM remains the most economical path from design to physical validation.

## SLA for Rapid Prototyping: The Detail Machine

SLA printing produces parts with surface quality that rivals injection molding. When your prototype needs to look and feel like the final product, resin printing delivers results that FDM simply cannot match.

### When SLA Excels

- **Visual prototypes** — Client presentations, investor demos, trade show models
- **Small, intricate parts** — Features as fine as 25–50 microns
- **Smooth surface finish** — Near-injection-mold quality straight off the printer
- **Dental and medical models** — Biocompatible and castable resins for specialized applications
- **Jewelry and miniatures** — Capturing fine textures, engravings, and organic shapes

### SLA Strengths for Prototyping

| Metric | SLA Performance |
|--------|----------------|
| Material cost | $50–150/L |
| Typical layer height | 0.025–0.1mm |
| XY resolution | 25–85 microns |
| Surface finish | Near injection-mold quality |
| Post-processing | Washing + UV curing required |

### SLA Limitations to Know

- Smaller build volumes (typically 120×68×155mm to 335×200×300mm)
- Resin parts can be brittle compared to thermoplastics
- UV sensitivity — parts degrade with prolonged sun exposure unless coated
- Post-processing is mandatory (IPA wash + UV cure adds 30–60 minutes)
- Material costs are 2–5× higher than FDM filaments

## Head-to-Head: FDM vs SLA for Prototyping

| Factor | FDM | SLA |
|--------|-----|-----|
| **Surface finish** | Visible layer lines | Smooth, near-production quality |
| **Dimensional accuracy** | ±0.2–0.5mm | ±0.05–0.15mm |
| **Material strength** | Strong (especially nylon, PC, CF) | Moderate (engineering resins improving) |
| **Speed (small parts)** | 1–4 hours | 1–3 hours |
| **Speed (large parts)** | 8–24 hours | Limited by build volume |
| **Cost per part** | Low ($1–15 typical) | Medium ($5–40 typical) |
| **Maximum part size** | Large (300mm+) | Medium (200mm typical) |
| **Fine detail** | Good (0.4mm nozzle limit) | Excellent (50μm features) |
| **Material options** | 50+ thermoplastics | 30+ specialty resins |
| **Post-processing effort** | Low | Medium (wash + cure) |

## Building an Effective Prototyping Workflow

The most productive prototyping teams don't choose between FDM and SLA — they use both strategically at different stages of development.

### Stage 1: Concept Validation (FDM)

Print rough models in PLA or PETG with 0.2–0.3mm layers and 15–20% infill. The goal is speed, not beauty. You're checking proportions, ergonomics, and basic geometry. Print multiple iterations in a single day.

### Stage 2: Design Refinement (FDM)

Switch to 0.12–0.15mm layers with functional materials (ABS, nylon, PETG). Test mechanical interfaces, snap-fits, thread engagement, and assembly clearances. This is where you catch tolerance issues before they become expensive.

### Stage 3: Pre-Production Validation (SLA)

Print final-look prototypes in SLA for stakeholder reviews, user testing, and marketing photography. Use engineering resins for parts that need to withstand handling. These prototypes should be indistinguishable from production parts at arm's length.

### Stage 4: Production Preparation

Use your validated prototypes to finalize specifications, create inspection fixtures (back to FDM for durability), and build confidence before investing in production tooling.

## Tips for Faster Prototyping Cycles

1. **Design for the technology** — Add draft angles for SLA, orient flat surfaces down for FDM, and include 0.3mm clearances for mating parts
2. **Batch your prints** — Fill the build plate with multiple design variants to test simultaneously
3. **Use the right resolution** — Don't print concept models at 0.05mm layers. Save precision for when it matters
4. **Keep a material library** — Stock 2–3 FDM filaments and 1–2 SLA resins so you're never waiting on shipping
5. **Outsource when it makes sense** — For complex parts or technologies you don't have in-house, [professional 3D printing services](https://fabricatte.com) can deliver production-quality prototypes in days
