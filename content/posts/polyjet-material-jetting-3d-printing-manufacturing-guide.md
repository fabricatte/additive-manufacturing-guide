If you've ever needed a 3D printed prototype that looks, feels, and functions like a final injection-molded product — complete with multiple materials, colors, and textures in a single build — PolyJet and Material Jetting technology might be exactly what you're looking for. While FDM and SLA dominate the desktop market, inkjet-based 3D printing occupies a powerful niche in professional manufacturing where detail, surface finish, and multi-material capability matter most.

In this guide, we'll break down how PolyJet and Material Jetting work, where they excel, and when this technology makes sense for your manufacturing workflow.

## What Is Material Jetting?

Material Jetting (MJ) is an additive manufacturing process that works remarkably like a standard inkjet printer — except instead of depositing ink on paper, it jets tiny droplets of liquid photopolymer onto a build platform. Each layer is immediately cured with UV light before the next layer is applied, building parts with extraordinary precision.

PolyJet, developed by Stratasys, is the most well-known implementation of Material Jetting. Other notable systems include 3D Systems' MultiJet Printing (MJP) and XJet's NanoParticle Jetting (NPJ) for metals and ceramics. While the branding differs, the core principle is the same: precision inkjet deposition of build material, one ultra-thin layer at a time.

## How PolyJet and Material Jetting Work

The process follows a straightforward sequence:

1. **Jetting:** Multiple print heads simultaneously deposit tiny droplets (as small as 14 microns) of liquid photopolymer resin across the build platform. Different heads can jet different materials at the same time.

2. **UV Curing:** Immediately after deposition, UV lamps mounted on the print head carriage cure and solidify each layer. This instant curing ensures dimensional stability and sharp layer definition.

3. **Layer Building:** The build platform lowers by one layer thickness (typically 14–32 microns) and the process repeats. Layer heights this thin are what give Material Jetting its trademark smooth surface finish.

4. **Support Removal:** Soluble or gel-like support material is removed after printing, either by hand, with a water jet, or in a chemical bath depending on the system and geometry.

The result is parts with some of the finest feature resolution and smoothest surfaces achievable with any 3D printing technology.

## Key Advantages of Material Jetting for Manufacturing

### Exceptional Surface Finish and Detail Resolution

With layer heights as low as 14 microns — roughly five times thinner than a typical FDM layer — Material Jetting produces parts with surfaces that rival injection molding. Fine text, thin walls, sharp edges, and intricate geometries print cleanly without the staircase stepping visible in other technologies. For consumer product prototypes, medical models, and presentation pieces, this level of finish often eliminates the need for post-processing entirely.

### True Multi-Material Printing in a Single Build

This is where Material Jetting truly separates itself from every other 3D printing technology. By combining multiple print heads jetting different materials simultaneously, PolyJet can create parts that blend rigid and flexible materials, combine opaque and transparent regions, or transition between different Shore hardness values — all within a single print job with no assembly required.

Imagine printing a complete over-molded grip with a rigid core and soft rubber-like exterior in one shot. Or a functional prototype with transparent windows, flexible gaskets, and rigid structural elements all built together. No other additive manufacturing technology can match this capability today.

### Full-Color and Texture Mapping

Modern PolyJet systems like the Stratasys J850 can produce over 500,000 color combinations directly during printing. Combined with texture mapping capabilities, designers can create photorealistic prototypes that are virtually indistinguishable from finished consumer products. Pantone-validated colors mean brand colors are reproduced accurately — critical for consumer goods, packaging prototypes, and design reviews.

### Speed for Detailed Parts

Because Material Jetting uses multiple print heads working in parallel across the full width of the build platform, it can produce highly detailed parts faster than competing high-resolution technologies like SLA or DLP. For batches of small, detailed parts, the wide-format jetting approach is particularly efficient.

## Limitations to Consider

No technology is perfect for every application, and Material Jetting has important trade-offs:

### Material Properties and Durability

PolyJet photopolymers are generally not as mechanically robust as engineering thermoplastics used in SLS, MJF, or even FDM. Parts can be brittle, and many PolyJet materials degrade over time with UV exposure, losing flexibility and becoming more fragile. This limits Material Jetting's suitability for long-term functional end-use parts exposed to sunlight or sustained mechanical loading.

### Cost Per Part

Material Jetting is among the most expensive 3D printing technologies on a cost-per-part basis. The photopolymer resins are costly, support material consumption can be significant (supports are jetted as a secondary material), and the industrial-grade machines carry high acquisition and maintenance costs. For production quantities, the economics rarely compete with SLS, MJF, or injection molding.

### Build Volume Constraints

Most Material Jetting systems have relatively modest build volumes compared to FDM or SLS platforms. While adequate for prototypes, medical models, and small components, large parts either won't fit or must be printed in sections and assembled.

### Support Material Waste

Every overhang and cavity requires jetted support material, which is discarded after printing. Complex geometries with deep internal channels can consume more support than build material, driving up both material cost and post-processing time.

## Ideal Applications for Material Jetting

Material Jetting excels in specific manufacturing scenarios where its unique strengths justify the cost:

### Product Design and Prototyping

This is the technology's sweet spot. When design teams need prototypes that accurately represent the look, feel, and function of a final injection-molded product — including material properties, colors, and textures — Material Jetting delivers results no other 3D printing technology can match. Faster design approvals and fewer revision cycles directly translate to reduced time-to-market.

### Medical and Anatomical Models

Surgeons and medical device companies rely on Material Jetting to create highly detailed, multi-material anatomical models for surgical planning, training, and device validation. The ability to print bone-like rigid structures alongside soft-tissue-like flexible regions in a single model provides invaluable tactile feedback that no screen-based visualization can replicate.

### Casting Patterns and Mold Masters

Material Jetting's smooth surfaces and fine resolution make it excellent for creating patterns used in investment casting, silicone mold making, and urethane casting workflows. A single PolyJet master can be used to create production-quality silicone molds for small-batch manufacturing.

### Consumer Electronics Prototyping

Smartphone cases, wearable devices, remote controls, and other consumer electronics benefit enormously from multi-material prototyping. Overmolded soft-touch surfaces, transparent display windows, and precise snap-fit geometries can all be validated in a single prototype build.

### Jigs, Fixtures, and Assembly Aids

While not as mechanically durable as SLS or FDM alternatives, Material Jetting is increasingly used for ergonomic assembly tools that combine rigid structural elements with soft contact surfaces to protect delicate components during manufacturing.

## How to Choose: Is Material Jetting Right for Your Project?

Consider Material Jetting when:

- **Visual fidelity matters most** — the prototype must look like the final product
- **Multi-material properties** are needed in a single part without assembly
- **Fine detail and smooth surfaces** are required without post-processing
- **Color accuracy** is critical for brand or design validation
- **Medical or anatomical models** need realistic multi-material representation

Look elsewhere when:

- **Mechanical durability** and long-term functional performance are primary requirements
- **Cost per part** must be minimized for production quantities
- **Large parts** exceed available build volumes
- **Outdoor or UV exposure** will degrade photopolymer materials
